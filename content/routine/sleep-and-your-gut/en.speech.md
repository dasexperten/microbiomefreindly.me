There are two stories people tell about sleep and the gut. One says your bacteria run your sleep. The other says a bad night wrecks your microbiome. Both are built on the same evidence, and that evidence is smaller and more interesting than either headline.

Start with what has been measured. In a population sample of seven hundred and twenty adults in Wisconsin, four things travelled with a less diverse gut: how much your sleep length varies from night to night, how long you lie awake after first falling asleep, low sleep efficiency, and simply rating your own sleep as poor. A smaller study followed twenty-six healthy young men wearing a wrist monitor for a month, and found the same direction.

Two designs, the same arrow. Worth something, but not a cause. People who sleep on a ragged schedule also eat on a ragged schedule, and food is the loudest influence on the gut there is.

Only one human experiment has pushed sleep on purpose and watched. Nine men, meals fixed by protocol, two nights with the lights out from a quarter to three until seven, against two normal nights. The balance between the two biggest bacterial groups shifted. Insulin sensitivity dropped. And the things that would have made a bigger story did not move: the overall structure of the community was unchanged, and so were the short-chain fatty acids in the stool. A nudge, in nine people, over forty-eight hours.

Now the part usually left out. Researchers looking for trials of probiotics on sleep found six, with a hundred and eighty people between them, mostly resting on questionnaires. A larger review of working adults said plainly there was not enough evidence to conclude anything about sleep. Nobody has shown you can sleep better by rearranging your bacteria.

So what do you do? Hold your rise time steady, because irregularity was one of the measures most consistently tied to lower diversity, and a fixed wake-up steadies your meal timing. Eat your fibre earlier: in a feeding study of twenty-six adults, more fibre predicted more deep, slow-wave sleep that night, while saturated fat predicted less of it and sugar predicted more waking. And leave a long gap before bed, because the daily rhythm of the gut is set by when you eat.

Hold the rise time and eat the fibre. Those are the moves with something behind them.
