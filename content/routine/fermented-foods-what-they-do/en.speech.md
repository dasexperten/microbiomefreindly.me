Let's talk about fermented foods, and let's start with what is actually in the jar.

Kefir, yoghurt, kimchi and sauerkraut are all made by microbes eating your food before you do. When those microbes are still alive at the moment you eat, the numbers are big. A survey of shop-bought fermented foods from the University of Nebraska found live yoghurt cultures ranging anywhere from under ten thousand to a billion per gram. Kimchi and sauerkraut came in a bit lower, in the tens of millions to hundreds of millions per gram. And an expert panel on probiotics puts the ceiling at up to a hundred billion lactic acid bacteria in one serving of a live yoghurt or kefir.

Now the interesting part. In twenty twenty-one, a team at Stanford ran a seventeen-week randomised trial. Thirty-six healthy adults, eighteen in each arm. One arm ate a high-fibre diet. The other worked up to six servings of fermented food a day, and actually hit it, averaging six point three servings.

In the fermented food arm, two things moved. The diversity of their gut bacteria went up, and stayed up for four weeks afterwards. And nineteen out of ninety-three inflammatory proteins in the blood went down. The high-fibre arm did neither.

Here is the twist, and it is the reason I am telling you this. The team sequenced the food as well as the stool. The new bacteria appearing in people's guts were mostly not the bacteria they had been eating. Their own resident community shifted. So this is not a story about swallowing bugs and having them move in. It is more likely a story about what the bugs left behind in the jar — the acids, the peptides, the broken-down sugars — arriving every day and changing the neighbourhood.

Which brings us to the practical part. Heat kills all of it. Pasteurised sauerkraut, soy sauce, vinegar, baked bread, most beer and wine — fermented, yes, but with no living microbes left by the time you eat them. If you want what the trial gave people, buy from the chiller, check the label says live, eat it most days, and rotate between the four rather than eating one.

And the honest caveat: this was thirty-six healthy people, and the study's own main endpoint did not move. Good habit, early evidence, not a treatment.
