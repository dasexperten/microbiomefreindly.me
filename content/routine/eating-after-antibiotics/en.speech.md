You take the last capsule, and the question arrives with it. Now what do I eat?

Start with the shape of the damage, because it is better news than most people expect. In Copenhagen, researchers gave twelve healthy men four days of three last-resort antibiotics at once, then followed their stool for six months. Bifidobacteria and the butyrate producers were knocked down. And then it came back. Composition was near baseline within one and a half months. For most of your gut community, this is weeks, not years. But there is a tail. Nine common species that every one of those men carried before treatment were still undetectable in most of them at one hundred and eighty days.

So what do you eat? Honestly, no trial has taken people finishing antibiotics, fed half of them a defined diet, and measured whose gut came back faster. That study does not exist.

What we have is next door to it. In mice, a fibre-poor diet made the collapse worse and the recovery slower. In healthy people over seventeen weeks, an arm that raised fermented food to about six servings a day gained microbiota diversity, and nineteen of ninety-three inflammatory blood proteins fell. The logic is plain, if not proven. The groups antibiotics knock down first live on fermentable plant carbohydrate. So feed the survivors what they eat. Legumes, oats, barley, onions, garlic, apples, cooked and cooled potatoes. A fermented food every day. And ramp up slowly, because that is how the trial was run.

Now the probiotic question, where the science argues with itself. In seventeen outpatient trials, antibiotic-associated diarrhoea hit eight percent on probiotics and nearly eighteen percent on control. But the largest single trial, in nearly three thousand hospital patients over sixty-five, found no difference at all. And a study that sampled the gut lining directly found probiotics made the return of a person's own bacteria markedly slower and persistently incomplete.

Those are not the same measurement. One is loose stools over weeks. The other is whose bacteria live in your gut over months. Nobody has run the trial that measures both in the same people.

The food is the low-risk half of all of this. And no cost beyond a few gassy days while your fermenters get back to work.
