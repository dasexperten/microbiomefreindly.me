Streptococcus mutans is a Gram-positive, sugar-fermenting, acid-tolerant bacterium that lives in the plaque on human teeth and is the single best-studied cause of tooth decay. That one sentence carries a century of evidence, so let me unpack it — and say out loud where the evidence gets thin.

## What it is

In 1924 a bacteriologist, J. K. Clarke, grew a streptococcus from a decayed tooth and named it *mutans*, because its oval cells looked to him like a mutant form of streptococcus [s2][s7]. The species name has held ever since; what has moved is everything around it. The group once called "S. mutans serotypes" was split by DNA comparison into separate species — four in all, two of them common in people, plus three that live in animals [s6]. Only serotypes c, e and f kept the name *S. mutans*; a fourth, serotype k, was added later [s11]. And in 2021 the phylum it belongs to was formally renamed from Firmicutes to Bacillota [s14] — so if an older textbook files it under Firmicutes, that is the same organism, not a different one.

## Where it lives

It needs a hard surface that does not shed. That is why newborns, who have no teeth, do not carry it. Caufield and colleagues followed 46 mother–child pairs from birth and watched mutans streptococci — the group this species leads — arrive at a median age of 26 months [s4] — a discrete "window of infectivity" that opens once there are teeth to live on. Eight children, 17 % of the group, were still free of it at a median age of 56 months [s4]. The likeliest source is the mouths of the adults who feed and kiss a toddler: among the children not yet colonised at one to two years, half were looked after by someone other than the mother, while every child in that age band who already had decay was cared for by the mother [s4].

Once established, it sits in the plaque biofilm, favouring the sheltered spots: in children's between-tooth plaque, serotype c — the common human type — made up 70 % of the counts just below the point where two teeth touch, but 39 % in the plaque away from that contact [s13]. It was also found frequently on tooth surfaces with no decay at all [s13] — remember that for later.

It can leave the mouth. Oral bacteria get into the bloodstream — bacteraemia — and *S. mutans* is a recognised cause of infective endocarditis, an infection of the heart valves [s2][s11]. Serotype k strains in particular have been detected, by DNA analysis, at a high rate in infected heart-valve specimens; they survive longer in blood because their surface is harder for antibodies to grip [s11].

## How it works — the mechanism

Three abilities, in order.

First, glue. When table sugar — sucrose — arrives, enzymes on the bacterial surface called glucosyltransferases split it and stitch the glucose halves into long, sticky chains called glucans. Those chains are the scaffold of plaque: they anchor the bacterium to the tooth and trap neighbours in with it [s2]. No sucrose, no glue.

Second, acid. The bacterium ferments sugars into lactic acid. The genome sequence tells you how seriously it takes this: the UA159 strain carries 2 030 936 base pairs and 1 963 open reading frames — predicted genes — and almost 15 % of that genome goes to hauling different sugars into the cell [s3]. When the acid pushes plaque below a "critical pH" — usually quoted as about 5.5 — the calcium phosphate mineral of enamel starts to dissolve [s5]. Dawes' point, and it matters, is that the number is not fixed: the critical pH rises or falls with how much calcium and phosphate are sitting in the fluid around the tooth [s5]. That is why a chalky early white spot can remineralise when the acid stops and saliva refills the mineral [s5].

Third, endurance. Most oral bacteria stop growing in that acid. *S. mutans* keeps going — it is aciduric as well as acidogenic — so each sugar hit tilts the community a little further toward the species that survive acid [s1][s2]. Loesche laid this out in 1986: the more often sugar arrives, the more often the pH crashes, the more the biofilm fills with acid-makers, the more mineral leaves the tooth [s1].

## Against us

Decay is the obvious harm, and its scale is not small. The Global Burden of Disease 2017 analysis counted 2.3 billion people with untreated decay in permanent teeth and 532 million children with untreated decay in milk teeth [s12]. One caveat, said plainly: those are counts of decay, not counts of this bacterium. The disease has other drivers — sugar frequency, saliva, fluoride, access to care — and other bacteria. In a 2012 sequencing study of young children, *S. mutans* dominated many mouths with decay but not all of them; in some, *S. sobrinus*, *S. salivarius* and *S. parasanguinis* carried the acid load with little or no *S. mutans* present [s9].

Beyond teeth, two harms are documented and one is under investigation. Endocarditis is documented [s2][s11]. Bacteraemia is documented [s11]. The third is the brain. Nakano and colleagues showed in 2011 that strains carrying a collagen-binding protein aggravated cerebral haemorrhage in mice, accumulated in the injured half of the brain, and were detected more often in haemorrhagic-stroke patients than in controls [s10]. That is a mechanism in mice plus an association in people. It is not a proof that the bacterium causes strokes in humans; the authors themselves call it a potential risk factor.

## For us

I went looking for a named study showing that *Streptococcus mutans* does something good for the person carrying it. I did not find one, and I will not invent one.

What the literature does support is narrower. In a balanced plaque community, at low numbers, it is a harmless resident — it turned up frequently on sound tooth surfaces in the 1998 Babaahmady study [s13], and the 2012 community analysis found bacterial diversity reduced in decay compared with health, with several species lost as decay advanced [s9]. It also makes bacteriocins called mutacins, which suppress competing streptococci such as *S. sanguinis*; that species fights back with hydrogen peroxide, and Kreth's 2005 experiments showed the two can coexist when they arrive together and exclude each other when one gets there first [s8]. That balance is real, but it is a benefit to the bacterium's own niche. Whether it protects you is not something anyone has shown.

## Why this matters

The mechanism gives you the lever. The bacterium cannot make its glue without table sugar — sucrose — and cannot hold the pH down — keep the plaque acidic — without frequent sugar [s1][s2]. Loesche's 1986 framing still stands: the ecology of plaque — which species thrive there — follows the diet, and the species that wins is the one you feed [s1]. What you do with that, and what this site can honestly say about any product that claims to shift it, comes later — after the evidence has been checked by the people whose job that is.
