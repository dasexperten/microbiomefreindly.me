Ask someone to name a bacterium that lives in their mouth, and you'll usually get Streptococcus, or a shrug. Almost nobody says Fusobacterium. But if you scraped the plaque off one tooth tonight and looked at it under a microscope, there's a good chance you'd be looking right at them. Long, thin rods, tapered at both ends like a spindle. That's the whole name. Fusus is Latin for spindle.

Fusobacteriota is a phylum. That's a very wide branch of the bacterial tree, not a single bug. And it's normal. When researchers built the first curated map of the human oral microbiome, they sorted six hundred and nineteen oral species into thirteen phyla, and this was one of the thirteen. It lives in dental plaque, in the groove where your gum meets your tooth, in your throat.

Here's what it does for us. Plaque isn't a smear. It assembles in order, early colonisers first, late anaerobes last, and this organism sits in the middle and binds to almost everyone. It's been described as building biological bridges that hold that community together. There's also older work showing that its cell wall switches on a natural antibiotic your gum makes itself, human beta-defensin two, within two to four hours, while a known gum pathogen didn't do that at all.

Now the caveat, out loud, because it matters more than the finding. That is not a health claim. Nobody has shown that more of this bacterium makes a mouth healthier.

And in the wrong place it's genuinely dangerous. In twenty-twelve, two labs independently found it enriched in colorectal tumour tissue. A year later, researchers worked out a mechanism: an adhesin called FadA binds a protein on human cells and switches on cancer-related signalling.

But here's the part the headlines skip. In twenty twenty-four, a team sequenced a hundred and thirty-five strains and found the tumour enrichment belongs to one sub-clade of one subspecies. Not to Fusobacterium as a whole. Most of what's in most mouths isn't that clade.

So there's no test to ask for, no diet shown to shift it, and no reason to attack your own plaque harder. The lesson isn't fear of a bacterium. It's that where a microbe lives can matter as much as which microbe it is.
