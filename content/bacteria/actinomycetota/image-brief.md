## Marika's template (§5)

**1. Slug (ASCII, language-neutral):**
actinomycetota

**2. Article title (EN) + one-line summary:**
"Actinomycetota: the phylum that guards infant guts and causes TB" — one of the four big gut phyla, formerly called Actinobacteria; the same family tree holds the bifidobacteria that feed a breast-fed baby's gut, the soil bacteria that gave us most of our antibiotics, and the bacterium that causes tuberculosis. Species, site and host decide the outcome, not the family name.

**3. Organism / structure in the preview:**
Bifidobacterium — the phylum's gut member, the one a reader of a gut portal actually carries. Not the tuberculosis bacterium, not soil threads. One organism, honestly drawn.

**4. Morphology, in words (truth, Magnus):**
Irregular rods with a forked, Y-shaped or club-shaped end — the "bifid" that gives the genus its name. Some single, some in pairs or short bent chains, a few with a knobbed tip. Non-motile: they lie where they are, they do not swim. No spores, no flagella, no spirals, no spheres. Thick-walled, so the edge of each cell reads solid and slightly waxy, not translucent. A field of them should look like a scatter of small forked twigs at rest, not like rice grains and not like fuzz.

**5. Preview mood (one line, warm science):**
Forked rods resting in a pale, creamy milk film on an ivory ground, morning light from one side, cells in navy-teal with the film carrying a single soft gold note where the light catches it — the infant gut being fed, quiet and abundant. Scale cue: the film's faint ripples and the fact that the cells are tiny against a soft, out-of-focus fold of gut lining behind them. Cells fill roughly the middle two-thirds and stay inside the safe band.

**6. Hero scene (one sentence, one moment):**
The scientist explains: Magnus at a microbiology bench lifting a culture plate towards the window light and looking at it steadily — the mood of someone who knows this family has two faces and is weighing which one he is looking at, calm rather than alarmed.

Breast milk contains sugars the baby cannot digest at all; Bifidobacterium infantis carries a dedicated set of genes that break those sugars down, and in doing so it fills the infant gut, makes acetate, and helps keep the gut wall tight against invaders — the mother feeds the bacterium so the bacterium protects the child.
