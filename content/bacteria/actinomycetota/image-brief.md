## Marika's template (§5)

**1. Slug (ASCII, language-neutral):**
actinomycetota

**2. Article title (EN) + one-line summary:**
"Actinomycetota: the phylum that guards infant guts and causes TB" — one of the four big gut phyla, formerly called Actinobacteria; the same family tree holds the bifidobacteria that feed a breast-fed baby's gut, the soil bacteria that gave us most of our antibiotics, and the bacterium that causes tuberculosis. Species, site and host decide the outcome, not the family name.

**3. Organism / structure in the preview:**
Amended 2026-09-16: *Streptomyces* — the phylum's soil member, named in the article as the source of most natural antibiotics and the reason for the name (*actino* ray, *mykes* fungus). The bifidobacterium entry in the same image wave already carries forked rods, so this card takes the phylum's other home; the forked gut member moves into the hero. Not the tuberculosis bacterium. One organism, honestly drawn.

**4. Morphology, in words (truth, Magnus):**
Soil member (preview and hero, left): *Streptomyces* grows as very thin, branching threads that spread through and over soil grains as a loose, mould-like mat; some threads rise upright into the air, and their tips divide into chains of small round-to-oval spores, straight or loosely coiled. The threads are far thinner than a soil grain and far longer than any single cell. No loose rods, no spheres in clusters, no spiral cells, no flagella.
Gut member (hero, right): *Bifidobacterium* — irregular rods with a forked, Y-shaped or club-shaped end, the "bifid" that gives the genus its name. Some single, some in pairs or short bent chains, a few with a knobbed tip. Non-motile: they lie where they are, they do not swim. No spores, no flagella, no spirals, no spheres. Thick-walled, so the edge of each cell reads solid and slightly waxy, not translucent. A field of them should look like a scatter of small forked twigs at rest, not like rice grains and not like fuzz.

**5. Preview mood (one line, warm science):**
Amended 2026-09-16: a crumb of damp soil seen very close, a fine mat of branching threads over its grains and a few upright threads ending in spore chains — earthy, still, nothing alarming. Scale cue: the soil grains read as smooth boulders beside the threads. Subject inside the safe band. World and light as in Marika's slot-and-focus block below.

**6. Hero mechanism (the answer, one clause) — Magnus, 2026-09-16:**
One family tree, opposite jobs: in soil its branching threads make most of the natural antibiotics in clinical use, while in the gut its forked rods live quietly on the lining — species and site decide the outcome, not the phylum's name.

**7. Three beats (first → next → outcome):**
(1) In a crumb of soil, thin branching threads with spore-chain tips grow through the grains; (2) the threads release small antibiotic molecules into the water film around them — medicine made in soil; (3) outcome: across the frame, on a smooth stretch of gut lining, the same family's forked, club-ended rods rest among other residents doing entirely different work — one thick-walled build, two species, two sites.

**8. The one number (keyFacts, source id):**
Members of this phylum produce about **two-thirds** of the naturally derived antibiotics in clinical use (`s2`, Barka et al. 2016). Shown only as proportion: of nine small antibiotic molecules gathered in the soil's water film, six rise from the branching threads and carry the accent; three drift in plain from beyond the frame edge. Never printed.

## For Lisa — the rest of what I was asked to say

**The single idea both images must carry:**
One family, two faces — and the face you meet in your own gut is the guardian. The card shows the soil face at work, the threads that gave us antibiotics; the article image walks from that soil to a gut lining, one family doing opposite work. Same warm ground, same navy-teal world, so the two read as one article and not two.

**The mechanism in one sentence, so Lisa knows what she is drawing:**
Breast milk contains sugars the baby cannot digest at all; Bifidobacterium infantis carries a dedicated set of genes that break those sugars down, and in doing so it fills the infant gut, makes acetate, and helps keep the gut wall tight against invaders — the mother feeds the bacterium so the bacterium protects the child. In soil, the phylum's Streptomyces threads make most of the natural antibiotics medicine uses.

**Priority:**
Bacteria entry = evergreen. No launch-week rush; correctness of the cell shape beats speed. If the threads come out as loose rods, or the gut rods lose their forked end, the frame is wrong for this article even if it is beautiful.

**Keyword for the file names (Jurgen's pattern):**
actinomycetota → `actinomycetota-preview` · `actinomycetota-hero` (plus `-og`, `-thumb` as Marika's §1 derives them).

**What the reader should feel, in one line:**
Preview — "so that is where most antibiotics come from, and it looks like quiet mould." Hero — "one family, and what it does depends on where it lives."
