Imagine a family reunion. One cousin has spent a century helping newborn babies digest their mother's milk. Another makes most of the antibiotics in your pharmacy. And a third causes tuberculosis, which killed about one and a quarter million people in 2023. They're all one family. The family's name is Actinomycetota.

Until 2021 it was called Actinobacteria, and you'll still see that name almost everywhere. Same group, new label. Nothing about the bacteria changed.

So what are they? A phylum, which is a very wide branch of the bacterial tree. Gram-positive, with DNA unusually rich in two of the four bases, and many of them grow as branching threads that look more like a mould than a microbe.

Where do they live in you? In your gut, they're one of the four big groups but only a small slice of the crowd. That slice is mostly Bifidobacterium. In a breast-fed baby, though, bifidobacteria often dominate, because they carry a set of genes built to digest the sugars in breast milk that the baby itself can't use. Mother makes a food her child can't digest, for a bacterium that can. In your mouth, the relatives are called Actinomyces, and they sit there quietly. On your skin, it's Cutibacterium acnes, which lives on everyone's face, acne or not.

What do they do for us? The best evidence is a mouse study in Nature. Certain bifidobacteria made acetate, and that acetate stopped a deadly E. coli toxin from crossing into the blood. The mice survived. But it was mice, and only some strains had the right genes. The strain matters more than the family.

And against us? Tuberculosis is the big one. About a quarter of the world's population is thought to carry it. Then diphtheria, nocardiosis, Whipple's disease. And acne, though here's the twist: people with acne don't have more of the bacterium than people without. It's a loss of balance, not an invasion.

One last caveat. Healthy people differ remarkably in their microbes. A stool test that says "Actinobacteria high" or "low" blends the helpful and the harmful into one number, and there's no evidence that pushing that number is a health goal. The real story lives at the level of species and strain. That's where any honest claim has to start.
