There's a bacterium living in the narrow, wet groove between your tooth and your gum, and it has no interest in sugar at all. Give it a doughnut, and it won't touch it. What it wants is protein. Yours. Its name is Porphyromonas gingivalis.

Here's the biology in one word: asaccharolytic. It can't ferment sugars. It grows on peptides, and it needs haem, the iron-carrying core of your haemoglobin, to live. So think about where a bug like that does well. Not on a sweet tooth surface. In a pocket that's inflamed, seeping and bleeding. That's its ideal home, and it's very good at creating one.

How? With enzymes called gingipains. One cuts protein chains after arginine, the other after lysine, and your own plasma protease inhibitors don't stop them easily. The arginine one switches on the kallikrein-kinin pathway, which gives you swelling, and switches on complement, which pulls in neutrophils. The lysine one chews up fibrinogen, which gives you bleeding. Swelling, neutrophils, bleeding on probing. Those are the classic signs a dentist looks for.

Now, here's the part that surprises people. Carrying this organism is not the same as having gum disease. In one study, it turned up in eighty-six percent of people with periodontal disease, and in twenty-three percent of perfectly healthy people. Another study, using a more sensitive method, found it in fifty-eight percent of healthy people. Those two healthy numbers disagree by more than double, and that's not a scandal. Different labs, different methods, different populations. The shape is what matters: it's commoner in disease, and it's still there in plenty of healthy mouths.

Does it do anything good for us? No. Nothing that's been demonstrated. No vitamin, no protection, nothing. The most honest positive thing you can say is that it can sit quietly for years without causing harm.

And the headlines about arthritis and Alzheimer's? There is real mechanism behind them. It's the only oral bacterium tested that can citrullinate proteins, which is the modification at the heart of rheumatoid arthritis. And in 2019, researchers found its enzymes in the brains of people who died with Alzheimer's. But that study combined human autopsy correlation with a mouse model, and most of the authors worked for the company developing the drug. Strong suspect. Not convicted. That distinction is the whole point.
