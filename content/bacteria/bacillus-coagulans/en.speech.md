Here's a bacterium that shouldn't work, and mostly does. It's called Bacillus coagulans, though microbiologists now file it under a newer name, Heyndrickxia coagulans, after a genome-based reclassification published in the International Journal of Systematic and Evolutionary Microbiology in twenty twenty-three. Same organism. Two correct names. Don't let a label confuse you.

What makes it unusual is the spore. Most of the friendly bacteria you've heard of — the ones in yoghurt — are fragile. Heat kills them. Time on a warm shelf kills them. This one folds itself into a dormant, armoured capsule and waits. That's why you'll find it in a chewing gum or a cereal bar or a lozenge, sitting there, alive, without a refrigerator anywhere in sight.

Now here's what most people get wrong. It doesn't move in. It isn't a resident of your gut or your mouth the way some bacteria are. A team in Milan, led by Duncan, showed that one strain survives the trip through your stomach and actually wakes up and germinates inside the human gut. But then it works, and then it leaves. Stop taking it and it's gone. So whatever benefit you get, you're renting it, not buying it.

What benefit? In the mouth, a systematic review and meta-analysis published in Frontiers in Oral Health in January twenty twenty-six pooled eight studies and found less Streptococcus mutans in saliva — that's the cavity bacterium. Encouraging. But I have to say the caveat out loud, because it's big: those studies disagreed with each other enormously. The direction is believable. The size of the effect isn't settled. And nobody has counted actual cavities.

In the gut, several randomised trials in irritable bowel syndrome found milder symptoms. Interesting detail, though: two of those trials measured inflammatory markers, and the markers didn't budge. So people felt better, but not, apparently, because inflammation went down.

And the honest other half — this same organism spoils warm, acidic, shelf-stable food. The armour that protects your probiotic also protects a spoilage bug. One trait. Two consequences. Both true.
