Say "E. coli" out loud and most people picture a recalled bag of spinach. That reflex is understandable, and it's mostly wrong. The bug in the headline and the bug in your colon are the same species — and the one in your colon has probably been there, quietly, for years.

E. coli is a rod-shaped gut bacterium that can breathe oxygen when there's any around and ferment when there isn't. Hold on to that flexibility, because almost everything it does for you, and everything it does to you, comes back to it.

It lives mainly in the large intestine, and it's nearly universal — more than nine in ten people carry it. But here's the surprise: you don't carry a hundred strains. Usually just one or two at a time, residents that stay for months, plus a few tourists passing through. Your E. coli is, in a real sense, yours.

What does it do for us? It's the main oxygen-tolerant organism in the gut, living there in symbiosis, and it can build vitamin K2. The caveat, said out loud: making that vitamin in your colon and actually absorbing it are two different questions, and the second isn't settled.

One strain does have a real trial behind it. In over three hundred patients with ulcerative colitis, the probiotic strain Nissle 1917 held remission about as well as the standard drug. But that's one strain, in one disease, against a drug rather than a placebo. It says nothing about the E. coli already living in you.

Now the other side, and it's heavy. E. coli is the leading bacterial cause of deaths linked to antibiotic resistance worldwide. It's behind most urinary tract infections. And the toxin-producing strains cause the outbreaks you read about — in Germany in 2011, contaminated sprouts led to more than three thousand eight hundred cases and fifty-four deaths.

One more thing. With that kind of food poisoning, don't reach for antibiotics. In children infected with the O157 strain, antibiotic treatment was linked to a much higher risk of serious kidney damage. Small study, wide uncertainty — I won't overstate it. But this is a doctor's decision, not a self-treatment one.

So both things are true at once. The lesson isn't to fear the E. coli inside you. It's to respect what a few of its relatives can do somewhere they don't belong.
