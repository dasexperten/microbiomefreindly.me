If you've ever had a stool test come back with a bar chart, the tallest bar was probably labelled Firmicutes. And the report may have told you that bar means you're wired for weight gain. Let's look at what the studies actually say.

First, the name. Firmicutes is now Bacillota. In 2021, Oren and Garrity validly published standardized names for forty-two bacterial phyla, and Firmicutes was one of them. The organisms didn't change. Only the label did.

Where does it live? Mostly in your large intestine. When Eckburg's team at Stanford sequenced the gut of healthy adults in 2005, the two dominant phyla were Firmicutes and Bacteroidetes, and most of what they found had never been grown in a lab. The lactobacilli that dominate a healthy vaginal microbiome belong here too. So does Staphylococcus aureus, which lives mainly in the nose.

What does it do for you? Here's the mechanism. Fibre reaches your colon undigested. Bacteria ferment it into short-chain fatty acids, and the main makers of butyrate, the fuel your colon lining runs on, are Bacillota. Louis and Flint's review lays that out. One of them, Faecalibacterium prausnitzii, calmed inflammation in human blood cells in Sokol's 2008 study, and its loss predicted Crohn's recurrence six months after surgery. The caveat, out loud: the treatment part of that study was in mice.

What does it do against you? The same phylum holds Clostridioides difficile. Lessa's surveillance study estimated around four hundred and fifty-three thousand infections and about twenty-nine thousand deaths in the United States in 2011 alone.

And that obesity ratio? It came from a 2006 Nature study of twelve obese volunteers. But when Magne's team reviewed the whole literature in 2020, the results were contradictory, and they concluded the ratio can't be used as a marker of obesity.

So a tall Bacillota bar isn't a diagnosis. It's a phylum that holds both your best butyrate makers and some of your worst pathogens, and the bar can't tell you which ones you've got.
