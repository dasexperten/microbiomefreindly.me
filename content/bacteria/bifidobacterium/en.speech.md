Ask someone to name a bacterium and you'll hear E. coli, or Salmonella, or something they once read on a chicken label. Almost nobody names the one that shows up first. Within days of birth, in a baby who's breastfed, a genus called Bifidobacterium takes over the large intestine so completely that it can be more than ninety percent of the bacteria living there.

So what is it? Picture a branched, Y-shaped cell that hates oxygen. That's why it thrives deep in the colon, and that's why keeping it alive outside the body is a manufacturing problem, not a slogan. The name comes from bifidus, which means forked.

Here's the part I find remarkable. Human milk oligosaccharides are the third most abundant solid thing in breast milk, and the baby can't digest them at all. Not one of them. They aren't baby food. They're microbe food. The mother makes a sugar she can't use, for an organism her child hasn't met yet, and bifidobacteria carry exactly the enzymes to break it down.

What does that buy us? In mice, bifidobacteria that make more acetate protected the animals against a lethal E. coli O157 infection, and the acetate stopped the Shiga toxin from crossing the gut wall. That's mice, one pathogen, particular strains. It shows a mechanism can work. It doesn't show your yoghurt does it.

One species in the genus, Bifidobacterium dentium, was isolated from dental caries and its genome is kitted out for surviving acid in the mouth. Probiotic strains have been cultured from the blood of very preterm babies, matched right back to the capsule. And the biggest trial we have, thirteen hundred and fifteen very preterm infants on a Bifidobacterium breve strain, found no benefit at all.

So here's my takeaway. Bifidobacterium matters enormously as biology. But benefit in the trials belongs to particular strains, in particular people, at particular doses. When a claim names a genus and not a strain, it's telling you about biology it borrowed, not a result it earned.
