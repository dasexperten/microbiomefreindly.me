Here's a bacterium with an unusual diet. It doesn't eat your food. It eats you — or rather, it eats the mucus lining your gut renews every day. Its name is Akkermansia muciniphila, which literally means the mucus-lover, and it's been living in most of us the whole time.

It was found in two thousand four, at Wageningen University, by researchers who diluted human stool until almost nothing was left alive and then offered the survivors just one thing to eat: mucin, the protein that makes mucus slippery. Something ate it. That something turned out to be common — a later count from the same group put it above one percent of all the bacterial cells in human stool. For a single species, that's a lot.

So is it eating you alive? No. And this is where it gets interesting. Mucus is renewed constantly, and trimming it seems to tell your gut to make more. In mice on a high-fat diet, published in the journal PNAS in twenty thirteen, giving them the live bacterium reversed fat gain and insulin resistance. Heat-killed cells did nothing — which tells you the effect is real biology, not wishful thinking.

There's been one careful human trial, in Nature Medicine in twenty nineteen. Forty overweight, insulin-resistant volunteers, thirty-two of whom finished three months. It was safe. Insulin sensitivity improved by about twenty-nine percent. But here's the part you won't hear in the headlines: people lost a little over two kilos, and that weight loss did not reach statistical significance. The trial was designed to test safety, not to prove weight loss. It's a reason to run a bigger study, not a reason to change your diet.

And the other side, said out loud. In gnotobiotic mice with a Salmonella infection, published in PLOS One, this same bacterium made the inflammation worse. And a study in Cell in twenty sixteen showed that when you take fibre out of the diet, the mucus eaters go after the lining in earnest, thinning the barrier until a pathogen can get through.

So the honest summary is this. It's not a hero and it's not a villain. It's a resident. What it does to you depends largely on what you feed it — and fibre is what keeps it off your lining.
