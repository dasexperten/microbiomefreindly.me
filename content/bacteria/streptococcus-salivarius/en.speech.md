Let me tell you about the first bacterium that ever lived in your mouth. It probably wasn't the one that causes cavities. In a small study, researchers swabbed twelve healthy newborns on their very first day of life, then again over the following year, and the organism they recovered most often was Streptococcus salivarius. Before the first tooth. Before the first meal. It was already there.

Here's what it is. Streptococcus salivarius is a normal, harmless resident of the human tongue and saliva. We're its only natural host. It doesn't live in soil, it doesn't live in animals, it lives on us. And when researchers dosed one volunteer with the probiotic strain called K12 and sampled sixteen sites in his mouth, the tongue carried the highest counts, saliva and cheek came next, and the tooth surfaces and gum pockets never turned positive at all. So this is a soft-tissue organism, not a plaque organism.

Now, what does it do for us? Three things. First, it makes antibiotics. Real ones. Many strains carry plasmid DNA that encodes bacteriocins, targeted protein weapons, including salivaricin A and salivaricin B. The probiotic strain K12 was the endpoint of roughly a forty-year search for a harmless bug that fights strep throat. Second, it's urease-positive, meaning it splits urea into ammonia, and ammonia buffers acid. Researchers believe that's one of the ecological brakes on tooth decay. Believe. Not know. Third, it simply takes up space, and it's numerically dominant on the tongue.

But now the caveat, said out loud. When you look at the actual human trials, they're thin. A 2019 systematic review found four randomised trials of K12 for sore throat, eighteen hundred and forty-six people, and judged every single one poor quality. For bad breath, it helped after a chlorhexidine rinse, and it did nothing at all in people who hadn't cleaned their tongue first. And the strain doesn't stay. It fades from the mouth within about three weeks.

One more thing you won't see on a label. This friendly bug has caused meningitis, when droplets from a doctor's mouth reached the spinal fluid during a lumbar puncture. Same organism. Different address. On your tongue it's a good neighbour. In your spinal fluid it's an emergency.
