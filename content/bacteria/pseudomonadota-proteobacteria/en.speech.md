You've probably met this group of bacteria under an older name. For decades the textbooks called them Proteobacteria. In twenty twenty-one the name officially changed to Pseudomonadota, and I want to be honest with you about what that means: nothing about the bacteria changed. It was a paperwork fix. The old name had never been properly published under the rules that govern bacterial names, because those rules didn't cover the rank of phylum at all until the committee voted to add it. Same organisms. New label. You'll see both for years.

So what are they? Think of a phylum as an enormous branch of the bacterial family tree, not a family and nothing like a species. This one holds E. coli, Salmonella, Helicobacter pylori, Pseudomonas, Neisseria, and the rhizobia that fix nitrogen in the roots of beans and peas. Two bacteria on the same branch can live completely different lives, and that single fact defuses most of what you'll read about this group online.

Here's what I find genuinely useful. In a healthy gut, this phylum is there, but it's a small share. A review in Trends in Biotechnology in twenty fifteen proposed that when that share climbs and stays up, it's a signature of a disturbed gut — not the cause of it, the signature. And the mechanism, laid out by Litvak and colleagues, is oxygen. A healthy colon lining is starved of oxygen, which keeps the gut airless and suits the strict anaerobes. Inflammation, or a course of antibiotics, lets more oxygen leak from that lining, and these bacteria, which can breathe oxygen when their neighbours can't, bloom on the difference.

That's why I'd be careful with a home gut test that flags your Pseudomonadota as high. There's no validated cut-off. The number shifts with how the sample was taken. And if the oxygen story is right, the rise is downstream of a gut lining in trouble, not upstream of it.

So it's a smoke alarm, not the fire. Presence is normal. It's the trend that carries the information, and the real question is always what happened to the gut lining that let the level climb.
