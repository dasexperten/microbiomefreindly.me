Ask most people what Lactobacillus is and they'll say "the good bacteria in yogurt." That's true. It's also the least interesting thing about it.

Lactobacillus is a group of bacteria with one trick: they eat sugar and turn it into lactic acid. That acid sours milk into yogurt and cabbage into sauerkraut. And everything this bacterium does for you, or to you, comes back to that acid.

Here's the first surprise. It barely lives in your gut. When researchers count bacteria in stool, lactobacilli come to about one in ten thousand, and in roughly a quarter of people they can't be found at all. Most are just passing through from food or the mouth. A few are real residents; most are tourists.

Where it really dominates is the vagina. In a study of nearly four hundred healthy women, most had a community run by one Lactobacillus species, keeping things acidic — the environment most pathogens struggle in.

The third place it lives is the least flattering. Lactobacilli settle inside tooth cavities. They don't start the cavity, but once it's there they move in, feed on sugar, and pour out acid that makes it deeper. Cavity-free children mostly don't carry them.

Now, what does it do for us? The best evidence is for antibiotic diarrhea. A Cochrane review pooled thirty-three trials in over six thousand children and found probiotics cut the risk of diarrhea by more than half. The caveat, said out loud: those trials used many different organisms, so that's evidence for the category, not for any one bottle.

And it doesn't work for everything. When nearly a thousand preschoolers with stomach flu got either Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG or a placebo, there was no difference at all.

Is it dangerous? For healthy people, almost never: in Finland it turns up in two of every ten thousand blood cultures. But in critically ill patients, genome sequencing has traced bloodstream infections straight back to the probiotic capsules they were given. In intensive care, this is a medical decision.

One more thing. In 2020, genome comparisons split the old genus into twenty-five genera. Lactobacillus rhamnosus is now Lacticaseibacillus rhamnosus. Same bug, new name.

So when you read a label, don't stop at the genus. Look for the strain, and for a trial on that strain for your problem. "Contains Lactobacillus" tells you about as much as "contains a mammal."
