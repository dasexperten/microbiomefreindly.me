Most bacteria get famous for what they do to people. This one got famous for going missing.

Its name is Faecalibacterium prausnitzii, and in a healthy adult it's one of the most common bacteria in the body. A 2013 review in Current Opinion in Microbiology put it at more than five percent of all the bacteria in the gut. It lives in the colon and basically nowhere else in us, and it can't tolerate oxygen at all. Oxygen isn't unhelpful to this organism. It's the end of it.

So what does it do for us? It makes butyrate. And butyrate isn't a small chemical footnote. In 2011, Dallas Donohoe and colleagues published work in Cell Metabolism showing that the cells lining the colon use bacterially produced butyrate as their primary energy source. Take the bacteria away and those cells sit there starving, running on empty, switching on autophagy. Add butyrate back and they recover. The gut lining has outsourced its own catering, and this bug is one of the caterers.

There's a second job too. In 2008, Harry Sokol's team found that people with Crohn's disease who had less of this species on the gut lining they'd had removed were more likely to relapse six months later. Then they tested it. The bacterium calmed immune cells down. And here's the odd part: it wasn't only the bacterium. The liquid it had been grown in, with the cells filtered out, blocked an inflammatory signal all by itself. It's secreting something.

Now the caveat, and I want to say it out loud. All of that human data is associational. The causal work is in mice and in cell lines. This bug is fragile, so when the gut gets inflamed and oxygen leaks in, it dies — which means a low reading might be a consequence of illness rather than its cause. The effects vary from strain to strain. And the name has moved twice: it used to be Fusobacterium prausnitzii, and in 2022 the very strain most of this research was done on got renamed Faecalibacterium duncaniae.

What no study here shows is that pushing this bacterium up makes anyone healthier. That's the trial we still need.
