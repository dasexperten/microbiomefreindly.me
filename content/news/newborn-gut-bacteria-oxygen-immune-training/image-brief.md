**Air should kill a newborn's first microbe. It breathes instead**

A *Cell* study reports that one fat built into the membrane of *Bacteroides fragilis* lets a strict
anaerobe outlast the oxygen of a newborn gut — and that the same fat tunes the immune cells that read
fats. Mice and molecules only; no human infants.

## 6. Hero mechanism (the answer, one clause) — Magnus, 2026-09-16

One fat built into its own outer membrane keeps protons from leaking out, so *Bacteroides fragilis* holds the charge it
runs on and survives — even respires — through the oxygenated window of a newborn gut; and the same fat is a molecule
the immune system reads, so it also calibrates the natural killer T cells growing up alongside it. Mice and molecules only.

## 7. Three beats (first → next → outcome)

1. In a young gut still carrying dissolved air, a scatter of fine air motes drifts against a short rod whose membrane
   rim is the tightest, cleanest edge in the frame — the fat sits in that rim as a line of small even nodes.
2. The sealed rim holds: protons stay in, the cell keeps its charge, takes up the drifting air motes and goes on
   working while neighbouring cells with slack, soft rims fade and stall.
3. Outcome: one of those same fat nodes is carried out of the membrane and held on the surface of a host cell, where a
   young natural killer T cell reads it and grows — a smooth, plain round cell taking its shape from the molecule.
