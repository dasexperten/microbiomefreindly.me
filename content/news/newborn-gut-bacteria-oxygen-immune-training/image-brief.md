## 2. Article title (EN) + one-line summary

**Air should kill a newborn's first microbe. It breathes instead**

A *Cell* study reports that one fat built into the membrane of *Bacteroides fragilis* lets a strict
anaerobe outlast the oxygen of a newborn gut — and that the same fat tunes the immune cells that read
fats. Mice and molecules only; no human infants.

## 3. Organism / structure in the preview

*Bacteroides fragilis* — and specifically **its outer membrane**, the wall itself. The membrane is the
subject, not a background detail.

## 4. Morphology, in words (truth — do not invent the shape)

Gram-negative **short rods**, blunt rounded ends, slightly uneven in length, pleomorphic — some near
oval, none spherical. Non-motile, no flagella, no spores. Single cells and loose pairs, a few lying
close but not a mat or a colony. Around each cell a soft polysaccharide coat, so the outline is a
little hazy rather than a drawn line. Under it the membrane reads as the **tightest, cleanest edge in
the frame** — that edge is the whole point of the story.

## 5. Preview mood — the zoomed world (one line, warm science)

A handful of short rods resting on a pale mucus film, lit warm from one side, close enough that the
rim of one cell is the sharpest thing in the picture — a smooth, sealed edge; and drifting in from
one side, thinning and dying out before it reaches the cells, a faint scatter of dissolved-air motes,
the hostile thing being held off. Ivory `#F1EADC` ground, cells in navy `#1B3856` with teal `#178B7A`
in their shading, **one gold `#C7A24B` note only** — a quiet warm gleam along a single cell's rim,
the fat that seals the wall. Scale cue: the mucus film and the soft shoulder of a villus fall away
behind, out of focus, so a reader feels a few microns, not a landscape. No people, no text, no
product, no invented packaging.

## 6. Hero mechanism (the answer, one clause) — Magnus, 2026-09-16

One fat built into its own outer membrane keeps protons from leaking out, so *Bacteroides fragilis* holds the charge it
runs on and survives — even respires — through the oxygenated window of a newborn gut; and the same fat is a molecule
the immune system reads, so it also calibrates the natural killer T cells growing up alongside it. Mice and molecules only.

## 7. Three beats (first → next → outcome)

1. In a young gut still carrying dissolved air, a scatter of fine air motes drifts against a short rod whose membrane
   rim is the tightest, cleanest edge in the frame — the fat sits in that rim as a line of small even nodes.
2. The sealed rim holds: protons stay in, the cell keeps its charge, takes up the drifting air motes and goes on
   working while neighbouring cells with slack, soft rims fade and stall.
3. Outcome: one of those same fat nodes is carried out of the membrane and held on the surface of a host cell, where a
   young natural killer T cell reads it and grows — a smooth, plain round cell taking its shape from the molecule.
