**1. Slug (ASCII, language-neutral) + article title**

`akkermansia-glp1-metabolic-syndrome-trial`
"The gut microbe that raised GLP-1 — and still missed its target". A 142-person trial of heat-killed *Akkermansia muciniphila* that did not improve whole-body insulin sensitivity, yet raised the body's own GLP-1 after a glucose drink.

**2. Organism / structure in the preview**

*Akkermansia muciniphila* in the mucus layer that lies over the intestinal lining. The organism and its address, nothing else.

**3. Morphology, in words (truth — this is the field nothing may be invented in)**

Oval short rods with rounded ends, standing alone or in pairs, sometimes in a loose small cluster. Not motile — no tails, no whips, no swimming. No spores, no chains, no spheres, no branching Y shapes. They sit inside a soft translucent film, not on a bare surface. Beneath that film, the lining rises in a rank of finger-shaped folds. The trial's cells were pasteurised — heat-killed — so they are drawn whole and unbroken but inert: smooth, still, a little paler, never burst, never in pieces.

**4–5. What the preview shows — a zoomed moment of this micro-world (preview mood)**

The lens is very close to a small stretch of gut wall, closer than any eye gets. A pale, half-transparent mucus film lies over the finger-shaped folds of the lining and catches a single soft light from one side, the way morning light sits on the surface of milk. The oval cells rest in that film at different depths — a few near the surface reading sharp, the ones further in going soft and pale as the film thickens over them. The film thins toward the lining underneath, and through that thinner place one small warm point of colour shows: a cell of the lining itself, the one that answers. That single point is the only accent in the frame.

Scale cue: the folds of the lining give the depth, so the eye understands it is looking at a wall from very near, not at a field of shapes floating in nothing.

Colour mood: the ground is ivory `#F1EADC` warming toward the light; the mucus film reads teal `#178B7A` at its palest; the cells are navy `#1B3856` softening to green `#2F7D55` where the light passes through them. One gold `#C7A24B` note only, at that single warm point beneath the film. No black ground, no glow, no neon. Warm, quiet, airy — a bench in morning light, not a screen in a dark room.

**6. Hero mechanism (the answer, one clause) — Magnus, 2026-09-16**

Heat-killed *Akkermansia* lying in the mucus against the gut lining nudged the lining's hormone cells into releasing more of the body's own GLP-1 after a glucose drink — an exploratory signal — while whole-body insulin sensitivity, the one thing the trial was built to test, did not move.

**7. Three beats (first → next → outcome)**

1. A dense drift of heat-killed oval cells — whole, unbroken, perfectly still — settles into the mucus blanket pressed against a short row of lining cells.
2. Where the mucus is thinnest, small protein molecules from those inert cells reach one hormone-making cell of the lining (an L cell), and it lets out a small burst of hormone packets.
3. Outcome: the burst stays a local event — beyond it, the wide field of surrounding tissue cells lies exactly as it was, unchanged. A nudge, not an effect.

**8. The one number (keyFacts, source id)**

**30 billion** pasteurised, heat-killed cells of *A. muciniphila* MucT in each daily dose (`s1`, Gut Microbes 2026). Shown only by quantity: the drift of inert oval cells on the left is far too dense to count, against the short row of lining cells it lies over and the single cell that answers. Never printed.

**The single idea both images must carry**

A very small resident on the other side of a thin wall can nudge the body's own hormone — and a nudge is a lead, not a cure. Quiet, close, honest. Both frames should feel like something worth looking at carefully rather than something being announced.

**Priority**

News — this week. The article is dated 2026-09-02 and is published with empty image fields.

**The mechanism in one sentence, for Otto's and Lisa's understanding**

*Akkermansia* lives in the mucus pressed right against the gut lining, close enough that something it carries or releases can signal across that thin film to the lining's hormone cells — which is why even heat-killed cells could raise the body's own GLP-1 after a glucose drink, although the one thing the trial was built to measure, whole-body insulin sensitivity, did not move at all.
