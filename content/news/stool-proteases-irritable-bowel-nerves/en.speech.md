If you've got irritable bowel syndrome, you know how the appointment ends. The camera finds nothing. The blood work is clean. And then someone tells you there's nothing wrong with your bowel. Well, a study published in the journal Gut in 2026 went looking for what we hadn't been measuring.

Here's what the researchers did. They collected stool from patients at centres in three countries: twenty-one people with the diarrhoea type of IBS, nine with the constipation type, and eighteen healthy volunteers. They spun the samples down and kept only the liquid — the fluid that actually bathes the gut wall.

Then they dripped that fluid onto living gut nerve networks. Not brain tissue. This is the nervous system inside the gut wall itself, the mesh of neurons that decides when your gut squeezes, when it secretes, and when it reports trouble.

And the nerves lit up. Fluid from both IBS groups fired those neurons far harder than fluid from the healthy people.

Now, why. In the diarrhoea group, the culprits were proteases — enzymes whose job is cutting other proteins. Block those enzymes, and the effect disappeared. They were working through a receptor called PAR-1, which sits on the cell surface and switches on when it gets snipped. In the constipation group, none of that applied. Same diagnosis on paper, different machinery underneath. The protein census agreed: forty-seven proteins differed between the diarrhoea samples and the healthy ones, several of them antibodies — the fingerprint of a quiet immune reaction.

But let's say the caveats out loud, because they're big. Those nerves were guinea pig nerves, not human ones. They were in a dish, not in a person, and a neuron lighting up under a microscope isn't a person doubled over at work. The constipation group was nine people. Nine. And nobody was treated — no protease blocker went into any patient.

So what changes today? Not your prescription. The conversation. There's now something physical and measurable in IBS stool that makes gut nerves fire. "Nothing wrong with your bowel" was never a finding. It was the limit of what we could measure.
