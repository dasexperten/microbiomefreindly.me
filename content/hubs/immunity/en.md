## What this system is

The largest immune organ in the body is the gut lining, and it is a single cell thick. On one side, a dense population of microbes; on the other, the body. Macrophages patrol that border. They swallow what crosses, and they decide — through the receptors on their surface — whether to sound an alarm or to stay quiet. Bacterial molecules such as short-chain fatty acids nudge them toward quiet; some cell-wall fragments push toward alarm.

Tolerance is the result of that constant negotiation. Regulatory T cells, many of them induced by specific gut bacteria, hold the peace. Germ-free animals show what happens without teachers: underdeveloped immune tissue, poor tolerance to food, exaggerated allergic responses.

## The three questions people ask

**Do probiotics boost immunity?** "Boost" is the wrong verb. A healthy immune system is calibrated, not maximal. Some strains change immune markers in trials; whether that translates into fewer infections is strain-specific and modest where it has been shown.

**Is inflammation caused by bacteria?** Inflammation is the immune system's normal response. Chronic, low-grade inflammation is associated with a leaky gut barrier, a fibre-poor diet and shifts in the microbial community — but the arrow of causation runs both ways.

**Can I fix allergies or autoimmunity through the gut?** Early-life exposure matters: farm children, pets and older siblings track with fewer allergies. For adults with established autoimmune disease, microbiome-based treatment is experimental.

## What the science can and cannot say yet

It can say: gut bacteria are necessary for a normally developed immune system; specific species induce regulatory T cells; macrophages read bacterial metabolites; tolerance is learned early.

It cannot yet say: which bacteria a given person is missing, whether adding them later restores tolerance, or whether measuring a cytokine tells you anything about your risk. Most of the mechanism is mouse data; the human evidence is observational.
