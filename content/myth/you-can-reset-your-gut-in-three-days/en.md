You have met this one in a supermarket aisle and in a podcast ad: three days of juice, or broth, or a powdered "gut protocol", and your microbiome starts over. Clean slate. New you. It is one of the rare health claims that arrives with a stopwatch attached, which makes it unusually easy to check — because the measurements exist, and they run the clock in both directions.

**Verdict: false.** Three days is genuinely enough to move a gut community — that part of the sales pitch is real. It is nowhere near enough to reset one, and "reset" is the word being sold.

## What three days actually does

Start with the strongest evidence that the claim is not simply nonsense. In 2014, David and colleagues recruited eleven volunteers [s1] — ten on each of two extreme diets, nine of them completing both arms [s1] — one diet composed entirely of animal products, one entirely of plants, each eaten for five consecutive days [s1]. The community moved, and it moved fast: differences in composition were apparent one day after a tracing dye showed the animal-based diet had reached the gut [s1]. Bile-tolerant organisms including *Alistipes*, *Bilophila* and [*Bacteroides*](/bacteria/bacteroidota/) rose on the animal diet, while the [Firmicutes](/bacteria/bacillota-firmicutes/) that ferment plant polysaccharides — *Roseburia*, *Eubacterium rectale*, *Ruminococcus bromii* — fell [s1].

Wu and colleagues had clocked the same speed three years earlier. In a controlled-feeding study of ten subjects [s2], composition changed detectably within 24 hours [s2] of a switch to high-fat, low-fibre or low-fat, high-fibre food.

So, yes. Three days of a serious dietary change will show up in a stool sample. If "reset" means "a different reading than last week", the claim is true, and trivially so.

## And how fast it undoes itself

Now the other direction, from the same paper. Subjects' gut microbiota reverted to their original structure two days after the animal-based diet ended [s1]. Two days. The change did not outlast the diet that caused it by even half a working week.

Wu's group tested the deeper layer and found it held. Enterotype identity — the broad *Bacteroides*-versus-*Prevotella* configuration a person sits in — remained stable across the whole 10-day study [s2] while day-to-day composition was shifting underneath it. Those enterotypes tracked long-term dietary patterns [s2]: the years of eating, not the week of it.

## The part that does not move

The most direct answer to "can I start over" comes from counting strains rather than species. Faith and colleagues sequenced the stool of 37 US adults [s3] across intervals reaching five years [s3] and found that, on average, 60 % of the roughly 200 microbial strains in an adult's intestine were retained over that five-year window [s3]. Extrapolating the decay curve, they concluded that most strains in a person are residents for decades [s3].

That is the number that decides this myth. Your gut is not a slate. It is a neighbourhood with a long-settled population, and three days changes what the residents are doing, not who they are.

## What a real disturbance looks like

If a juice protocol cannot reset a gut, what can? Antibiotics are the strongest disturbance anyone has deliberately applied to healthy volunteers, and even they underdeliver on the word.

Palleja and colleagues gave 12 healthy men [s4] a four-day course [s4] of three last-resort antibiotics — meropenem, gentamicin and vancomycin — and followed them for six months [s4]. The community crashed, bloomed with pathobionts, then rebuilt: back to near-baseline composition within about 1.5 months [s4]. But nine common species present in every subject beforehand were still undetectable in most of them after 180 days [s4].

Dethlefsen and Relman had watched a milder version in three people over ten months [s5] spanning two courses of ciprofloxacin. Diversity dropped within three to four days of starting the drug [s5]. Communities began returning about a week after each course ended [s5], but the return was often incomplete [s5], and by the end of the study each person's gut had stabilised at a composition altered from where it started [s5].

Read those two together and the picture is not a reset. It is a community that resists being pushed, springs most of the way back when pushed hard, and keeps a mark where it was pushed hardest.

## Why "reset" is the wrong word

A reset implies a factory state to return to. Your microbiome has no factory state. The only baseline it holds is the one you have been assembling since birth — and if you are unhappy with your gut, that baseline is precisely the thing you were hoping to leave. Returning to it is what a three-day protocol reliably does [s1].

The honest version of the claim is narrower and more useful than the one on the label. Diet changes the gut community quickly, and the community holds the change for as long as you hold the diet. That is not a disappointment; it is the mechanism. Fibre feeds fermenters, and fermenters bloom while the fibre keeps arriving. Stop, and within about two days you are back where you began [s1].

## What to take from it

Judge the claim by its clock. Three days is long enough to change a measurement and too short to change a population. Nothing in the literature above says a short protocol is harmful, and nothing in it says the effect survives the protocol. If someone is selling you seventy-two hours, ask what they expect to be true on hour seventy-three — and whether they have measured it.

Three days is a reading on a lab test, not a renovation. The renovation is the fourth month of eating differently, and nobody sells that in a bottle.
