Here is a sentence you have probably heard, or said. It's not my teeth. It's coming from my stomach.

It is the most durable explanation for bad breath in circulation, and it survives because it feels obvious. The mouth opens onto a tube, the tube ends in a stomach, and air comes out of the mouth. Something must be rising.

The verdict is: partly true, and the partly is very small.

A Belgian breath clinic examined four hundred and ninety-one patients, with an ear-nose-throat specialist, a gum specialist, and when needed a psychiatrist in the room. Oral causes accounted for eighty-seven per cent of them. The digestive tract accounted for one per cent. One patient in a hundred, in a clinic people reach only after everything simpler has already failed.

So where is the smell actually made? Bad breath is mostly one family of molecules, the volatile sulfur compounds. Hydrogen sulphide and methyl mercaptan make up around ninety per cent of the sulfur in mouth air, and most of it comes off the back third of the tongue. That surface is fissured and papillated. It holds a coat of shed cells, saliva sediment and food debris. Underneath that coat, the oxygen runs out, and anaerobic bacteria strip the sulfur off proteins and let it go as gas. The raw material is your own dead cells.

What about Helicobacter pylori? A meta-analysis of twenty-one studies did find bad breath about two point eight five times more likely in infected people. But a study that bothered to look inside those patients' mouths found the smell tracking their decayed teeth and their hygiene scores, not the infection. The authors said plainly that they could not pin it on the bacterium.

Here is why this matters. If you believe the smell comes from below, everything you try points downward. Digestive remedies, elimination diets, endoscopies. Meanwhile the tongue goes unscraped.

Clean the back of the tongue. Have the gums looked at. Treat the mouth as the default suspect, because statistically it is. If the smell survives a genuinely clean mouth, then look outward: the nose and sinuses first, then blood-borne causes, and only then the stomach.

That order is not prejudice. It is the order the numbers were counted in.
