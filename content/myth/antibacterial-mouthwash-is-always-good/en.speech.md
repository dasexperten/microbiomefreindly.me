Here is a claim you have probably believed. Bacteria cause cavities and gum disease, so fewer bacteria must be better, and a rinse that kills nearly all of them must be best of all.

The verdict is false. Not partly true. False.

A broad antiseptic rinse does not choose. It cannot tell the organism eroding your enamel from the one on your tongue that your circulation depends on. It removes both.

Here is the measurement. In a study in Scientific Reports in 2020, thirty-six healthy adults rinsed twice a day for seven days with a placebo, then repeated the schedule with chlorhexidine. Their bacterial community did not simply shrink. It tilted. And the mouth left behind was chemically different: more acidic, buffering less well, with more lactate and free glucose. That is precisely the environment the cavity bacteria like.

Now the part that surprises people. Your own cells cannot turn nitrate into nitrite. Bacteria on your tongue do that for you, and that nitrite becomes nitric oxide, which relaxes your blood vessels.

In a trial in Free Radical Biology and Medicine, nineteen healthy volunteers rinsed with a chlorhexidine antiseptic for a week. Their oral nitrite production fell by ninety percent. Their blood pressure rose by two to three and a half millimetres of mercury, and each person's rise tracked their own nitrite fall — within one day.

Now the honest caveat, because a myth check that only cuts one way is not a myth check. This does not happen to everyone. A randomised crossover in seventeen young women blocked the same pathway and found no blood pressure change at all. The disruption is reliable. The consequence is not.

And antiseptic rinsing genuinely works, at what it works at. A Cochrane review of fifty-one studies found a large reduction in dental plaque — but a gum benefit the reviewers called not clinically relevant, and a large increase in tooth staining as the price.

So it is a treatment, with an indication and a stopping point. After gum surgery. When brushing is impossible. When someone has named the problem and the end date. Not a bottle you reach for every morning because your mouth feels ordinary.

A mouth is not a wound. Its residents are working for you. The goal was never fewer bacteria — it was the right ones, in the right places, in the numbers they belong in.
