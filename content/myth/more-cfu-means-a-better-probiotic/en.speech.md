Here is a claim you have almost certainly seen on a supplement shelf: more CFU means a better probiotic. Colony-forming units. The bigger the count, the better the product.

The verdict is: partly true. And the partly is where all the interesting biology lives.

Let me give you the strongest case for it first, because it is a real one. A dose-ranging trial in two hundred fifty-five adult hospital patients starting antibiotics. Everyone got the same blend of two Lactobacillus strains. The only thing that changed was one capsule a day, or two. Each capsule held fifty billion cells, so the high arm swallowed a hundred billion a day. Antibiotic-associated diarrhoea turned up in fifteen and a half percent of the two-capsule group, twenty-eight percent of the one-capsule group, and forty-four percent on placebo. For Clostridioides difficile, the gap was even wider. That is a genuine dose-response.

Now the other half. Change the strain, change the outcome, and the curve goes flat. Five hundred fifty-nine children hospitalised with acute watery diarrhoea got Lactobacillus rhamnosus GG at two doses a hundredfold apart. Both worked better than rehydration alone. Neither worked better than the other. Not a little better. Not at all.

The same thing happened in ulcerative colitis. Seventy-five people, a regular dose and a double dose of one strain for a month. Both groups improved. Nothing separated them.

Why? Because the count you swallow is rarely the bottleneck. Surviving stomach acid is. Having a mechanism for the thing you actually want is. Whether your own resident bacteria will let a newcomer settle is. Once enough cells arrive to pull the lever, extra cells have no second lever to pull.

That is why the literature keeps landing on the strain rather than the number. Two hundred twenty-eight randomised trials, reviewed together, found that efficacy is strain-specific and disease-specific. Within one species, some strains prevented antibiotic-associated diarrhoea and others did nothing at all.

And then there is the blunter problem. The number may not even be true. Ten products labelled as one Limosilactobacillus reuteri strain, tested inside their shelf life: only four matched their labels. Two grew no colonies whatsoever.

So read the strain code, not the size of the number. A big number with no named strain behind it is not a stronger product. It is a stronger advertisement.
